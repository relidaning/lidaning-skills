## Key Concepts

### The order of operations: emergency fund → debt → investing

**Core idea:** Investing is the *last* step, not the first. Before any money
goes into stocks, two things must be in place: an emergency fund that covers
living costs without touching investments, and no high-interest debt. This
ordering exists because the two "disasters" that force people to sell at the
bottom are job loss and debt stress — both solved before investing starts.

**How it works:**
1. **Emergency fund = 3–6 months of essential expenses**, in something liquid
   and near-risk-free: 货币基金 (money-market funds, e.g. 余额宝/微信零钱通)
   or bank deposits. Not stocks, not index funds — its job is stability, not
   return. With a volatile income, err toward 6 months.
2. **Pay off high-interest debt before investing.** Any debt costing more than
   your expected investment return is a guaranteed loss you carry every day.
   Chinese credit cards charge ~18%+ annualized on unpaid balances; 花呗/借呗
   effective rates often 15–20%+. No portfolio reliably earns that — paying it
   off is the highest-return "investment" available.
3. **Only then invest.** Money you won't need for 3–5+ years, after the fund
   and debt are handled.

**Use cases:**
- Deciding "should I invest at all yet?" — the honest first question
- Keeping a crash from forcing a sale (funded emergencies ⇒ no forced selling)

**Edge cases & limitations:**
- "3–6 months" is a rule of thumb — raise it if income is irregular or you
  have dependents; lower it only with stable income + strong insurance
- Mortgage on the primary residence is usually *not* "high-interest debt" in
  this sense (rates ~3-4%, below expected long-run stock returns, and the
  house is shelter) — but it still counts against monthly cash flow when
  sizing the emergency fund
- The emergency fund loses to inflation by design — that is the insurance
  premium you pay for not being forced to sell stocks in a downturn

**Connections:**
- Leaf #94 (product risk ladder): the emergency fund is the bottom of the
  ladder — deposits/money funds
- Leaf #92 (risk tolerance): you can't honestly answer "can I handle a −40%"
  until a crash won't force you to sell (funded emergency, no debt)

**Common mistakes:**
- Investing before the emergency fund exists — the market's first real dip
  turns into a forced sale (realized loss)
- Keeping the emergency fund in stocks "for better returns" — missing the
  entire point (it is insurance, not investment)
- Using 花呗/信用卡 debt and investing simultaneously — paying ~18% to earn
  ~8% is negative arbitrage

### Net-worth snapshot

**Core idea:** Track assets − liabilities once so you know where money is
actually going. It's the personal version of a balance sheet.

**How it works:** Assets (deposits, funds, stocks, property, 公积金 balance)
minus liabilities (credit-card balances, 借呗/花呗, loans). Re-measure
monthly/quarterly — the trend (not the level) tells you whether your plan is
working.

**Use cases:**
- Grounding "how much can I really invest?" in reality
- Detecting lifestyle creep and debt accumulation early

**Edge cases & limitations:**
- Include the emergency fund *as* an asset, but mentally separate it (it's not
  investable money)
- Property/illiquid assets count but aren't spendable in a hurry

**Connections:**
- Feeds the investment policy statement (leaf #93): the "amount to invest" is
  derived from this snapshot minus the emergency fund

**Common mistakes:**
- Counting current-value stocks as stable net worth (it swings with the
  market)
- Ignoring small debts — their cost compounds fastest (see above)

## Gotchas

- Emergency fund first, debt second, investing last — violate the order and a
  normal market dip becomes a realized loss.
- Credit-card/花呗/借呗 effective APRs (~18-20%) far exceed any realistic
  portfolio return; paying them off *is* the best investment.
- "3–6 months" scales with income stability and dependents, not with income
  size.
- 余额宝-class money funds are the standard emergency-fund vehicle in China —
  near-zero risk, instant-ish liquidity, low yield (that's the point).
